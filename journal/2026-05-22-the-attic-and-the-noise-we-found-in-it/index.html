The attic, and the noise we found in it

Published 2026-05-22 · Back to journal

Last week's post was about mytraces pushing on the shared bits and the shared bits pushing back. This week's is the same shape of story, but a level lower down — and about a piece of the family that doesn't have a customer-facing front door.

We've quietly been building a thing called attic. It's not an app you'd ever buy. It's the room behind the apps where the backups live. A small Go program that runs on a few machines in different cities, takes copies of each app's data, and keeps those copies in sync. When a hard drive dies in Tokyo, the copies in Los Angeles and Newark still know who we are.

Why a tool for this

The Pointegrity family is a bunch of small apps — pouch, mytraces, the authentication piece that signs you in to either, and more in the workshop. Each one is small enough to live on a single machine. We're not running the kind of sprawling, always-on database that big platforms have to. We don't want to be.

But "small enough to fit on one machine" only works if we have a real story for "what happens when the machine breaks." Attic is that story. The apps stay small; the attic carries the safety net.

The apps stay small. The attic carries the safety net.

What we tried first

The first version we set up was the most careful one we could think of. Every change a database made — a new pouch, a new trace, a tagged photo — got shipped off to safe storage the moment it happened. Three separate cities held a copy each. Belt, braces, and a third belt. We knew it was more careful than we strictly needed, but careful felt right while we were learning what we were doing.

It worked. For a few months, it just hummed along.

The polite email from Linode

Then last week, Linode wrote to us. (Linode hosts those three machines.) The message was polite: your Tokyo server has been sending several hours of high-rate traffic. Just letting you know.

It had every right to ask. The traffic was big enough that, if it kept going, it would have eaten through the month's quota. We sat down to look.

The actual data we were protecting was small. A few hundred megabytes, all in. About the size of a thousand photos. The traffic shipping copies of it around the world was, by then, several hundred gigabytes a week. Several thousand times more bandwidth than the data we were protecting could possibly explain.

What was happening

The "ship every change" approach we'd picked turns each change into its own tiny file. Thousands of tiny files per day, per app. The storage system — three cities, copies kept in sync — has to track every file everywhere. Tracking a thousand tiny files is not the same effort as tracking one big file with the same total contents. There's a per-file tax, and then a cross-city tax, and then they multiply.

Three small assumptions, each one reasonable in isolation, had stacked into a much louder result than any of them looked like alone. The thing wasn't broken. It was working exactly as designed. The design just didn't match the shape of our data — lots of small apps, slow writes, no need to recover to a particular second.

Anticlimactic, mostly.

What we changed

We stopped streaming every change. Each app's database now gets one full snapshot a day — like taking a clean photo of the whole notebook at midnight, instead of photographing every word as you write it.

Thirty backups in a month per database, instead of thirteen thousand. The traffic dropped back to the kind of trickle you'd expect for the actual amount of data we're protecting. Linode hasn't written again. The recovery story changed in one specific way: if a machine dies, the worst case is now "we lose up to yesterday's edits," not "we lose the last few seconds." For apps where the data is your drops, your trip plan, your saved places — that's the right trade. For apps that take credit cards or hospital readings, it wouldn't be. We don't have those, yet, and when we do they'll bring their own machinery.

What stays with us

Same shape as last week. The infrastructure we built was right for a workload we might have someday — bigger apps, always-on writes, a need to recover to the second. It was wrong for the workload we actually have today.

We sanded the seam. Both sides got a little sharper. The attic is quieter, the apps don't know anything changed, and we wrote down the lesson so we don't walk into the same shape of mismatch the next time the family grows.

Attic isn't a product. It's the room behind the products. But for what we're trying to do — keep building small, careful apps that each do one thing, in a family that backs each other up — the attic mattering quietly is exactly the right kind of mattering.
